BOQ & Pricing

A Practical Field Guide to Reading a Tender BOQ

A step-by-step walkthrough for locating, understanding and cross-checking a Bill of Quantities before you quote a single rate.

A Bill of Quantities (BOQ) is the document your entire bid is built on — and misreading it is one of the most expensive mistakes a bidder can make. It's usually buried inside a larger tender package alongside dozens of other pages, easy to skim past or misinterpret under deadline pressure. Here's how to actually read one properly, step by step.

Step 1: Find the actual schedule, not just the headline value

Every tender notice states an estimated or approximate value up front — but that headline figure is not the same document as the BOQ or Schedule-B itself. The schedule is usually a separate annexure listing individual items (or, for percentage-rate tenders, referencing a Schedule of Rates). Locate that specific document before doing anything else. Bidders who price only against the headline value, without ever opening the actual schedule, routinely miss items, wrong units, or a total that doesn't match what they assumed.

Step 2: Identify the pricing structure

Before you can price anything, confirm which structure the schedule uses:

  • Item rate — a list of individual items, each with a quantity, asking you to fill in your own rate per item.
  • Percentage rate — a single ask for a percentage above or below a published estimate or Schedule of Rates, rather than item-by-item pricing.
  • Lump sum / EPC — one fixed total for the whole scope (or per defined package), with no line-by-line quantities to price against.

Each requires a completely different pricing approach, so getting this identification right first is non-negotiable.

Step 3: Check units and quantities carefully

For an item-rate schedule, confirm the unit of measurement for every single item — per square metre, per cubic metre, per running metre, per kilogram, per each — since misreading a unit (pricing per metre when the schedule means per square metre, for instance) silently multiplies your rate wrong. Also watch for quantities marked provisional or nominal: these are estimates the department expects may change once actual measured work is done, and they shouldn't be treated with the same confidence as a firm, fixed quantity.

Step 4: Watch for pre-filled columns

Some schedules include the department's own estimated rate in one column, right next to the blank column you're meant to fill in with your quoted rate. It happens more often than you'd expect: a bidder accidentally enters a rate in the wrong column, or worse, copies the department's estimated rate back to them instead of their own competitive price. Always confirm which column is actually being scored before you submit.

Step 5: Cross-check the schedule total against the tender's headline value

Once you've priced the schedule (or noted its stated total, for a percentage-rate tender), compare that figure against the tender notice's own "estimated value." These two numbers frequently don't match, and the mismatch usually has a real explanation — the headline figure might include contingencies, taxes, or scope outside the priced schedule, or the schedule might only cover part of a larger project value. Don't assume they should be identical; understand why they differ before you finalise anything.

Step 6: Note provisional sums and prime cost items

Many schedules include a provisional sum or prime cost (PC) item — a placeholder allowance for work or materials that aren't fully specified yet (a nominated subcontractor's scope, for example, or a material whose exact specification is still being finalised). These are typically fixed allowances passed through the contract rather than items you compete on — don't build your margin expectations around them the way you would a normal priced item.

Common mistakes

  • Pricing from the headline value alone — never opening the actual schedule document and pricing based on the tender notice's summary figure instead.
  • Missing a unit mismatch — quoting per running metre against a quantity that's actually in square metres, or similar.
  • Treating provisional quantities as fixed — building a tight margin around a quantity the department has explicitly flagged as subject to change.
  • Ignoring provisional sums when comparing totals — assuming your competitively-priced total should equal the tender's headline value, without accounting for pass-through allowances.

Quick checklist before you quote

  1. Have you located the actual BOQ/Schedule-B, separate from the tender notice's headline figure?
  2. Have you confirmed whether it's item rate, percentage rate, or lump sum/EPC?
  3. Have you checked every item's unit of measurement, and flagged provisional/nominal quantities?
  4. Have you confirmed which column is the one being scored, if an estimated rate is shown alongside it?
  5. Have you reconciled your schedule total against the tender's headline estimated value, and understood any difference?
  6. Have you identified any provisional sums or PC items and excluded them from your competitive margin?
